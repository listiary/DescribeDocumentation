3) Publication-ready rewrite (human, not spec)
Simplifying the world, one list at a time.

A few years ago I realized most of my wikis were lying to me.

Runbooks lived as paragraphs. Taxonomies hid inside headings. Checklists turned into prose the moment they grew up. I kept forcing lists into pages, and the result was drift: hard to scan, hard to trust, hard to maintain.

Listiary is my bet that the medium is the message. If your knowledge is hierarchies, timelines, taxonomies, and checklists, your wiki should make those first-class — not afterthoughts.

What Listiary is

A lists-first wiki where everything is a nested, interactive list you can expand, filter, sort, and act on. Think of incident timelines you can replay, roadmaps that sort by priority, taxonomies with attached sources, and runbooks with real checkboxes — all versioned, forkable, and shareable.

The moment that started it

I was trying to trace an incident timeline across six tools: tickets, chat logs, dashboards, a wiki, a doc, and a spreadsheet. The “page” I wrote to summarize it felt dead on arrival. What I needed was a living tree of events: expandable, filterable by system, with timers, sources, and annotations. That’s Listiary’s north star.

Why these choices (and what I’m saying “no” to)

Why a custom language (DML) instead of Markdown?
Markdown is great at paragraphs; it’s vague about structure. I needed predictable trees. Describe Markup Language (DML) is a tiny DSL with a formal grammar so lists are unambiguous and machines can help. Authors keep a readable syntax; tools get a reliable AST. Tradeoff: you learn a new (small) syntax. Payoff: powerful behaviors and safe transformations.

Why C# + ANTLR for the compiler?
ANTLR gives me a robust parser generator; C# gives performance, tooling, and testability. The compiler is a separate tool so other stacks can reuse it. Tradeoff: mixed-language codebase. Payoff: a clean boundary and long-term maintainability.

Why vanilla JS + PHP + SQL for the platform?
Self-hostable, minimal dependencies, easy to reason about, easy to fork. Tradeoff: fewer framework conveniences. Payoff: small attack surface, simple ops, approachable for more contributors.

What I’m not building (on purpose)
No “everything editor.” No vague plugin that can rewrite the universe. Lists are the core primitive; everything we add should make structured knowledge truer and faster, not heavier.

What’s working today (prototype)

Interactive lists: expand/collapse, sort, filter, checkboxes, timers.
Inline editing with drafts & forking: try ideas without breaking the canonical list.
Responsive UI: phone to desktop without weirdness.
DML toolchain: formal grammar, compiler, tests.

Where it’s going (near-term, human outcomes)
Public plugin API: so people can add behaviors (e.g., “SLA countdown,” “citation collector”) without touching core.
Import/export (Markdown, JSON, OPML): bring your world in; take it with you.
Auth, roles, moderation: because lists are collaborative and the internet is the internet.
Federation (opt-in): communities host their own spaces; you can follow lists across servers — like RSS meets outliners.
1.0 means: stable DML, stable plugin surfaces, documented extension points, and performance that makes large trees feel instant.
